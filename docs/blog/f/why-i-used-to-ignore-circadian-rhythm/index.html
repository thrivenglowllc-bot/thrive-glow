Why I Used to Ignore Circadian Rhythm...

January 30, 2026 · Theresa

AND WHY I WILL NEVER DO IT AGAIN!

For a long time, I didn’t think circadian rhythm really mattered.

I thought hormones were mostly about what you eat, what supplements you take, or whether you’re “doing enough” workouts. Sleep felt important, sure—but circadian rhythm? That felt like one of those wellness buzzwords that sounded interesting but not life-changing.

At the time, I was a full-time corporate girlie while also going back to school to finish my BA… and then my Master’s. My days were long, my nights were even longer, and my schedule was whatever I could survive. Late-night studying, scrolling or Netflix to decompress, caffeine to function, rinse and repeat.

I was tired—but I thought that was just life.

What I didn’t realize then was that my body was constantly fighting against its natural rhythm. And that quiet mismatch? It was affecting everything—my hormones, energy, mood, digestion, and overall health.

Once I finally understood circadian rhythm, it completely changed how I approach wellness—not rigidly or unrealistically, but in a way that actually works for busy women with full lives.

What Is Circadian Rhythm (In Simple Terms)?

Your circadian rhythm is your body’s internal 24-hour clock. It tells your body when to:

  • Wake up
  • Feel alert
  • Digest food efficiently
  • Release hormones
  • Wind down
  • Repair and restore while you sleep

This clock is primarily regulated by light and darkness, especially sunlight in the morning and darkness at night.

When your circadian rhythm is aligned, your body works with you.
When it’s off, your body is constantly playing catch-up.

And here’s the key part most people don’t realize:

Your hormones run on this clock.

How Circadian Rhythm Affects Your Hormones

Cortisol (Your Stress Hormone)

Cortisol is supposed to be highest in the morning to help you wake up and feel energized, then gradually lower throughout the day.

When circadian rhythm is disrupted (late nights, inconsistent sleep, screen exposure at night):

  • Cortisol can stay elevated all day
  • Or spike at night when it should be low

This can lead to:

  • Feeling “tired but wired”
  • Anxiety
  • Trouble sleeping
  • Weight gain, especially around the midsection

Melatonin (Your Sleep Hormone)

Melatonin rises in the evening when it gets dark and helps you fall asleep.

Blue light from screens, late-night work, and inconsistent bedtimes suppress melatonin production, making it harder to fall asleep and stay asleep.

Poor melatonin = poor sleep
Poor sleep = hormone chaos

Insulin & Blood Sugar

Your body is more insulin-sensitive earlier in the day. Eating late at night or constantly snacking disrupts this rhythm.

Circadian disruption has been linked to:

  • Blood sugar imbalances
  • Increased cravings
  • Fatigue after meals
  • Increased risk of metabolic issues

Female Hormones (Estrogen & Progesterone)

Your reproductive hormones rely heavily on:

  • Quality sleep
  • Stress regulation
  • Consistent daily rhythms

When circadian rhythm is off, it can contribute to:

  • PMS
  • Irregular cycles
  • Hormonal acne
  • Worsened perimenopause symptoms

This is why sleep and daily rhythm matter just as much as nutrition.

Circadian Rhythm Impacts More Than Hormones

This is where things really clicked for me.

Circadian rhythm doesn’t just influence hormones—it impacts nearly every system in your body:

  • Gut health: Digestion, enzyme release, and gut motility follow circadian patterns
  • Immune function: Your immune system does most of its repair while you sleep
  • Brain health: Memory consolidation and mental clarity depend on proper sleep cycles
  • Inflammation: Disrupted rhythms are associated with higher inflammation levels

Your body heals, detoxifies, and restores itself on a schedule. When that schedule is constantly disrupted, symptoms begin to appear—often quietly at first.

Why This Matters for Busy Women

Most wellness advice isn’t designed for women who:

  • Work full-time
  • Are in school
  • Are raising families
  • Are juggling everything at once

I lived that life. I know what it feels like to sacrifice sleep because it feels like the only “me time” you have.

The goal isn’t perfection.
It’s supporting your biology in realistic ways.

How to Support Your Circadian Rhythm (Without Overhauling Your Life)

1. Morning Light Exposure

Getting sunlight in your eyes within 30 minutes of waking helps:

  • Set your cortisol rhythm
  • Improve energy
  • Support better sleep at night

Even 5–10 minutes outside helps.

2. Consistent Sleep & Wake Times

Your body thrives on consistency more than duration.

Going to bed and waking up around the same time— even on weekends— helps regulate hormones and energy.

3. Eat Earlier When Possible

Your metabolism follows your circadian rhythm.

Prioritizing:

  • A nourishing breakfast
  • Balanced meals earlier in the day
  • Lighter dinners

can support blood sugar and hormone balance.

4. Create an Evening Wind-Down Routine

Your nervous system needs cues that it’s safe to rest.

This can be as simple as:

  • Dimming lights
  • Reducing screen time
  • Stretching
  • Reading
  • Gentle prayer or journaling

These small habits tell your body it’s time to shift gears.

5. Be Gentle With Yourself

Circadian rhythm isn’t about control—it’s about alignment.

Some seasons will be messy. Some nights will be late. What matters is the overall pattern, not perfection.

I used to think circadian rhythm was optional. Now I see it as foundational.

When you support your circadian rhythm, you’re not just “sleeping better”—you’re supporting your hormones, metabolism, mental health, gut, and long-term wellness.

For busy women, especially, this can be one of the most impactful—and free—tools for feeling better in your body.

Your body wants to work for you. Sometimes it just needs the right rhythm.
