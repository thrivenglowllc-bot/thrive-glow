Should you be eating more Fiber?

August 7, 2026 · {{owner_name}}

When I saw a specialist for my unbearable stomach pain, I was told I needed to eat more fiber. I thought to myself, how will fiber help stomach pain when I do go to the bathroom regularly? All my test results came out fine, and the doctor couldn't tell me what was wrong. I basically walked out of the office paying a co-pay, told I was fine even though I didn't feel fine, and to eat more fiber. I thought, "another appointment where I got NO answers."

A few years later, I was going through my Integrative Health Certification, and that is where I learned fiber is one of the most overlooked nutrients for digestion, metabolic health, and hormone support. So why couldn't a doctor tell me this? When I was told to eat more fiber, it sounded like something they say on routine when test results come back fine. By the way, I did increase my fiber intake, and it did not help my stomach pain.

Fiber does much more than help you have regular bowel movements. It feeds beneficial gut bacteria, supports healthy blood sugar and cholesterol levels, promotes fullness, and helps the body eliminate waste—including metabolized hormones.

Understanding the different types of fiber and gradually adding more fiber-rich foods to your meals can have a meaningful impact on your overall health.

What Is Fiber?

Dietary fiber is a type of carbohydrate found primarily in plant foods. Unlike sugars and starches, fiber is not completely broken down and absorbed in the small intestine. Instead, it travels into the large intestine, where it may add bulk to stool or be fermented by gut bacteria.

Fiber is commonly divided into two categories.

Soluble fiber

Soluble fiber absorbs water and forms a gel-like substance in the digestive tract. This can slow digestion, support steadier blood sugar levels, and help lower LDL cholesterol.

Sources of soluble fiber include:

  • Oats and oat bran
  • Chia seeds
  • Basil seeds
  • Ground flaxseed
  • Beans and lentils
  • Apples
  • Pears
  • Citrus fruits
  • Avocados
  • Psyllium husk

Insoluble fiber

Insoluble fiber does not dissolve in water. It helps add bulk to stool and supports the movement of food and waste through the digestive tract.

Sources of insoluble fiber include:

  • Leafy greens
  • Cruciferous vegetables
  • Nuts and seeds
  • Brown rice
  • Quinoa
  • Whole grains
  • Vegetable skins
  • Wheat bran

Many plant foods contain a combination of soluble and insoluble fiber, which is why eating a varied diet is more valuable than relying on one food or supplement.

How Fiber Supports Your Gut

Your digestive system contains trillions of microorganisms collectively known as the gut microbiome. These microorganisms help break down food, produce certain vitamins and metabolites, support immune function, and communicate with other systems throughout the body.

Certain fermentable fibers act as prebiotics, meaning they provide nourishment for beneficial gut bacteria.

When gut bacteria ferment fiber, they produce compounds called short-chain fatty acids, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Research suggests that these compounds help support the intestinal barrier, influence immune activity, and participate in glucose and lipid metabolism.

Butyrate is particularly important because it provides energy for the cells lining the colon. A well-supported intestinal lining helps maintain the gut barrier—the protective boundary that controls what moves from the digestive tract into the bloodstream.

Fiber can also help support microbial diversity. However, different fibers feed different organisms. Oats, beans, berries, onions, garlic, asparagus, flaxseed, resistant starch, and other plant foods each contain different fibers and plant compounds.

This is one reason why plant diversity matters just as much as total fiber intake.

Fiber and Regular Bowel Movements

Fiber supports healthy elimination by increasing stool bulk, holding water in the stool, or changing the speed at which material moves through the digestive system.

A systematic review found that fiber supplementation can improve chronic constipation, with psyllium showing particularly promising results when used consistently.

However, more fiber is not automatically better for every person.

Adding large amounts too quickly may cause:

Adding large amounts too quickly may cause:

  • Bloating
  • Gas
  • Abdominal pressure
  • Cramping
  • Changes in bowel movements

These symptoms do not always mean that fiber is “bad” for your gut. They may mean that the amount was increased too quickly, fluid intake is inadequate, or a particular type of fiber is poorly tolerated.

People with irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, gastroparesis, intestinal narrowing, or chronic constipation may need individualized guidance before dramatically increasing fiber.

The Gut-Hormone Connection

Your gut and hormones constantly communicate with one another. Hormones can influence digestion, motility, and the microbiome, while the gut microbiome can influence how certain hormones are metabolized.

One area of research focuses on the estrobolome, a collection of gut bacterial genes involved in estrogen metabolism.

After estrogen is used by the body, the liver helps process it so it can be eliminated through bile, stool, and urine. Certain intestinal bacteria can produce enzymes that reactivate conjugated estrogen, potentially allowing some estrogen to be reabsorbed rather than eliminated.

The relationship is complex and is still being studied, but the gut microbiome appears to play a role in regulating circulating estrogen availability.

Fiber may support healthy estrogen elimination in several ways:

  1. It promotes regular bowel movements.
  2. It can bind to compounds in the digestive tract.
  3. It may reduce the amount of time waste remains in the colon.
  4. It supports a healthier gut microbial environment.

Research has proposed that fiber may reduce the reabsorption of estrogen by increasing intestinal transit and promoting fecal estrogen excretion.

Older controlled studies have also found that certain high-fiber diets can alter circulating estrogen concentrations, although results vary depending on the source and type of fiber.

This does not mean that fiber “balances hormones” by itself or that everyone needs to lower estrogen. Hormonal health depends on ovarian function, age, body composition, thyroid health, liver function, stress, sleep, nutrition, medications, and many other factors.

Fiber is best viewed as one part of the body’s larger hormone-metabolism and elimination system.

Fiber, Blood Sugar, and Insulin

Although fiber is classified as a carbohydrate, it behaves differently from refined carbohydrates.

Soluble and viscous fibers slow stomach emptying and carbohydrate absorption. This can reduce how quickly glucose enters the bloodstream after a meal.

For example, eating a piece of fruit generally produces a different blood sugar response than drinking fruit juice. Whole fruit contains fiber and requires more digestion, while juice delivers sugar with much less fiber.

Supporting steadier blood sugar may also support hormone health indirectly. Frequent blood sugar spikes can increase insulin demand, and chronically impaired insulin sensitivity can interact with appetite, inflammation, reproductive hormones, and fat storage.

Fiber-derived short-chain fatty acids may also influence glucose regulation and metabolic signaling, although individual responses differ and research remains ongoing.

An easy way to build a more balanced meal is to combine:

  • Fiber-rich carbohydrates
  • Protein
  • Healthy fats
  • Non-starchy vegetables

For example, instead of eating plain white rice by itself, pair a smaller serving of rice with salmon or chicken, roasted vegetables, avocado, and lentils or edamame.

Fiber, Hunger, and Weight Management

Fiber-rich foods generally require more chewing and take longer to digest. Many also contain a high volume of food for relatively few calories.

Fiber may help support fullness by:

  • Slowing digestion
  • Increasing meal volume
  • Supporting steadier blood sugar
  • Influencing appetite-related signals
  • Feeding gut bacteria that produce metabolic compounds

This does not make fiber a weight-loss medication. However, meals built around vegetables, beans, berries, seeds, and minimally processed whole grains are often more satisfying than meals based primarily on refined flour and sugar.

Consider the difference between eating a small pastry and eating a bowl containing eggs, avocado, sautéed vegetables, and berries. The second meal provides more protein, water, micronutrients, and fiber, which may help you remain satisfied longer.

Fiber and Cholesterol

Soluble fiber can bind to bile acids in the digestive tract. Because bile acids are produced from cholesterol, the body may need to use additional cholesterol to replace what is eliminated.

This is one reason soluble fibers—particularly beta-glucan from oats and barley and viscous fibers such as psyllium—are associated with healthier cholesterol levels.

Higher fiber intake has also been consistently associated with better cardiovascular outcomes. A large meta-analysis found that each additional 7 grams of daily fiber was associated with an approximately 9% lower risk of cardiovascular disease and coronary heart disease.

Associations do not prove that fiber alone caused the reduction. People who eat more fiber may have other health-supportive habits. Still, the consistency of the evidence supports including fiber-rich foods as part of a heart-healthy diet.

Fiber, Inflammation, and Immune Health

A large portion of the immune system interacts with the gastrointestinal tract. The intestinal lining must tolerate beneficial organisms and food particles while responding appropriately to harmful pathogens.

Short-chain fatty acids produced from fiber fermentation can influence immune signaling and help support the gut barrier. This may be one pathway through which a fiber-rich diet contributes to healthier inflammatory regulation.

It is important to avoid describing individual foods as instant “anti-inflammatory” treatments. Inflammation is influenced by sleep, stress, infections, exercise, environmental exposure, body composition, medications, genetics, and the overall dietary pattern.

Eating a variety of fiber-rich plant foods is one practical way to support the systems involved in healthy immune and inflammatory responses.

Fiber and Long-Term Health

Research consistently associates higher dietary fiber intake with a lower risk of several chronic diseases.

A 2024 meta-analysis found that higher fiber consumption was associated with lower all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer-related mortality.

Fiber-rich dietary patterns may contribute to long-term health by supporting:

  • Blood sugar regulation
  • Healthy cholesterol levels
  • Bowel regularity
  • Microbial diversity
  • Weight management
  • Intestinal barrier health
  • Removal of waste products
  • Greater intake of vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and phytonutrients

Fiber should not be viewed as an isolated miracle nutrient. It is also a marker of eating more beans, vegetables, fruits, seeds, nuts, and whole grains—foods that contain many other beneficial compounds.

How Much Fiber Do You Need?

Fiber recommendations are generally based on age, sex, and calorie needs. A commonly used guideline is approximately 14 grams of fiber for every 1,000 calories consumed.

For many adults, that works out to roughly:

  • Around 25 grams per day for many women
  • Around 30 to 38 grams per day for many men

Individual needs vary, and tolerance matters.

Rather than immediately aiming for a perfect number, start by noticing how many fiber-rich foods appear in your meals throughout the day.

Examples of Fiber-Rich Foods

Approximate fiber amounts can vary by product, size, and preparation method.

Fruits

  • 1 cup raspberries: about 8 grams
  • 1 medium pear with skin: about 5–6 grams
  • 1 medium apple with skin: about 4–5 grams
  • 1 cup blackberries: about 7–8 grams
  • ½ avocado: about 5 grams

Vegetables

  • 1 cup cooked artichoke hearts: approximately 9 grams
  • 1 cup cooked broccoli: approximately 5 grams
  • 1 cup cooked Brussels sprouts: approximately 6 grams
  • 1 medium sweet potato with skin: approximately 4 grams
  • 1 cup cooked carrots: approximately 4–5 grams

Beans and legumes

  • 1 cup cooked lentils: approximately 15 grams
  • 1 cup cooked black beans: approximately 15 grams
  • 1 cup cooked chickpeas: approximately 12 grams
  • 1 cup cooked split peas: approximately 16 grams

Nuts, seeds, and grains

  • 2 tablespoons chia seeds: approximately 8–10 grams
  • 2 tablespoons ground flaxseed: approximately 4 grams
  • 1 cup cooked quinoa: approximately 5 grams
  • 1 cup cooked oatmeal: approximately 4 grams
  • 1 ounce almonds: approximately 3–4 grams

Fiber is one of the most powerful nutritional tools for supporting the gut and overall health.

It feeds beneficial gut bacteria, promotes short-chain fatty acid production, supports regular bowel movements, helps regulate blood sugar and cholesterol, and participates in the elimination and metabolism of hormones.

But fiber is not a quick fix. It works best as part of a consistent dietary pattern that includes a variety of vegetables, fruits, beans, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains.

Start slowly, pay attention to your body, and focus on diversity rather than perfection. A few additional grams of fiber each day can become a meaningful investment in your digestion, hormones, metabolism, and long-term wellness.

This article is intended for educational purposes and is not a substitute for personalized medical care. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional if you have persistent digestive symptoms, unexplained changes in bowel habits, blood in the stool, severe abdominal pain, or a diagnosed gastrointestinal condition.
